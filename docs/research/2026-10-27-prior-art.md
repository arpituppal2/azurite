# Prior art and what it forces

Written 2026-10-27, before any training run. Every number here is a published
number with a URL. Where a number is a derived estimate rather than a
measurement, it says so.

The purpose of this document is to stop the project from making claims the
literature has already settled, and to locate the claim the literature has *not*
settled — because that second thing is the only defensible PhD contribution
available at this budget.

---

## 1. What the models we intend to beat actually cost to train

### 1.1 Laya's English checkpoint is a 2-trillion-token encoder

The Laya model card states its English checkpoint is **ModernBERT-large plus a
two-layer decision head, 421M parameters total**, context 512. The multilingual
checkpoint is **mmBERT-base + head, 322M**, context 1,024. The typed-decisions
checkpoint is ModernBERT-large + head, 421M.
([laya model card](https://huggingface.co/convaiinnovations/laya);
[typed-decisions card](https://huggingface.co/convaiinnovations/laya-typed-decisions);
our own safetensors header count: 421.3M / 321.9M / 421.3M)

So the comparison target is not "a 421M encoder" — it is ModernBERT-large,
whose published recipe is:

- **149M parameters (base)**, 22 layers; **395M (large)**, 28 layers.
- **~2T tokens**: 1.7T at sequence length 1,024, then 300B for context
  extension to 8,192.
- Base trained from random initialization; **large initialized from the trained
  base**, i.e. the published large recipe is *not* a from-random 395M run.
- GLUE aggregate: **88.4 (base), 90.4 (large)**, against **88.1 /
  91.4** for DeBERTaV3-base/large (183M / 435M).
  ([ModernBERT paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.13663v2);
  [HF announcement](https://huggingface.co/blog/modernbert))

The multilingual checkpoint is **mmBERT-base: 22 layers, 307M total (110M
non-embedding), 256k vocabulary, ~3T tokens across 1,833 languages** (the paper
and repo describe phases of 2.0–2.3T + 600B + 100B; the phase split is not
self-consistent across public materials, the ~3T headline is).
([mmBERT paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.06888v1);
[mmBERT repo](https://github.com/jhu-clsp/mmBERT))

**Forced consequence.** The current PLAN.md §3.2 prices "Laya parity" at
400M × **100B** tokens = $420. That token count is wrong by a factor of 20. The
honest parity target is 395M × 2T tokens ≈ **4.74×10²¹ FLOPs**, and the base
initialization it sits on top of is another 1.52×10²¹. See §5 for the corrected
arithmetic.

### 1.2 What comparable runs actually achieved per H100

Three published data points convert FLOPs into GPU-hours, and they agree:

| run | params | tokens | reported cost | implied achieved | implied MFU |
|---|---:|---:|---|---:|---:|
| ModernBERT base ([issue #236](https://github.com/AnswerDotAI/ModernBERT/issues/236)) | 149M | 1.7T | 194 h on 8×H100 = 1,552 GPU-h | 272 TFLOP/s | 27.5% |
| mmBERT base ([repo](https://github.com/jhu-clsp/mmBERT)) | 307M | 3T | ~40 days on 8×H100 = 7,680 GPU-h | 199 TFLOP/s | 20.1% |
| French ModernBERT-style ([Antoun et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.08716v2)) | ~400M | 1T | 1,300 H100-h | 513 TFLOP/s | 52%* |

\* The French figure implies 52% of the 989 TFLOP/s *sparsity-inclusive* H100
peak, i.e. marginally above the dense peak — so either its model is smaller than
400M, or the figure measures something slightly different. It is the optimistic
end of the range, not the central case.

**Two independent runs at 20.1% and 27.5% MFU** is the number to budget from.
PLAN.md currently assumes 40%. That assumption is replaced by a measured range
of **20–28%**, and every cost below is reported as a range over it.

### 1.3 Jev discloses nothing quantitative

TypeSafe describes Jev as a "System One" model returning typed probabilistic
decisions, citing "a new model architecture", a "parallel sampler", and
"Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions (RLCD)". No parameter count, no
corpus size, no token budget, no architecture, no context length.
([TypeSafe announcement](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev))

**Forced consequence.** Any "line-for-line comparison with Jev" has a column of
cells that must read **not disclosed**. They will not be filled with estimates.
The only Jev quantities that can be compared are its *published benchmark
scores*, and those carry a protocol defect of their own — see §7.

## 2. Has from-scratch ever beaten pretrained at matched scale?

This is the question the project's claim depends on, so it gets its own section.

**There is exactly one clean precedent, and it is domain-specific.**
[PubMedBERT](https://arxiv.org/html/2007.15779v6) trained a BERT-base-scale model
**from random weights with a vocabulary learned from its own domain corpus**
(14M PubMed abstracts, 3.1B words, 21 GB, 62,500 steps, ~5 days on 16×V100) and
beat BioBERT — a model that starts from general-domain pretrained BERT — on a
natural-language classification task: **HoC 82.32 vs 81.54 micro-F1**, BLURB
aggregate **81.16 vs 80.34**. The margin is 0.78 F1 points, and the setting is a
high-resource specialised domain where the vocabulary mismatch is severe.

**Near-misses that are not the claim.** ModernBERT-base (149M, from random, 2T
tokens) reached **88.4 GLUE** against **88.1** for the larger, pretrained
DeBERTaV3-base — but it used a modified OLMo tokenizer, so it is not a strict
no-pretrained-assets result, and GLUE-base is not a 421M comparison. MosaicBERT
(137M, from random, **83.2 GLUE-dev in 4.6 hours on 8×A100**) reuses the
standard BERT tokenizer. ([MosaicBERT](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.17482))

**The literature does not forbid the claim, and it does not support it.** There
is no published general-domain result of a strict from-scratch model beating a
strong 421M pretrained encoder. There is also no published parameter or token
threshold below which this is impossible — the "critical model size" hypothesis
in [Beyond Chinchilla-Optimal](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/sardana24a.html)
is not confirmed by its own small-model experiments out to 10,000 tokens per
parameter.

**What the literature does establish is the data-efficiency of transfer.**
[Scaling Laws for Transfer](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2102.01293)
estimates that a pretrained 40M model fine-tuned on 300k characters matches a
from-scratch model of the same size only after roughly **1,000×** as much
downstream data, and that from-scratch models become *data-limited* — accuracy
stops improving with more parameters — while pretrained models keep improving at
a reduced slope. That is the mechanism behind every negative result in this
section.

## 3. Scaling laws: what they do and do not predict

| law | predicts | measured range |
|---|---|---|
| [Chinchilla](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.15556) | pretraining **loss** | ~20 tokens/param compute-optimal |
| [Muennighoff et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.16264v5) | loss under data constraint | ≤4 epochs ≈ loss-neutral; half-life ~15 repeats |
| [Beyond Chinchilla-Optimal](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/sardana24a.html) | loss, with inference cost | improves to 10,000 tokens/param at 150M |
| [Hernandez et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2102.01293) | transfer scaling | ~1,000× data-efficiency factor |

**Every one of them predicts loss or transfer, none predicts decision quality.**
No published law maps (N, D) from-scratch pretraining to *accuracy, calibration,
or risk-at-coverage on a fixed multi-task decision suite*, with the compute axis
shared with a pretrained reference point. That absence is this project's opening.
It is also the only claim in reach: a loss scaling law is already published, so
reproducing one locally would be a lab exercise, not a contribution.

## 4. Byte-level vs subword: the claim in PLAN.md §5 is wrong as written

PLAN.md §5 says a byte tokenizer "multiplies sequence length by roughly 3–4×
versus a 32k BPE, so it costs 3–4× the compute for the same text". The measured
literature does not support the second half.

[ByT5](https://arxiv.org/html/2105.13626v3) measured **4.1 UTF-8 bytes per
SentencePiece token** on its mC4 mix (language range 2.5–9.0). That is the
sequence-length ratio. But for **parameter-matched** models it reports only
**~1.2× forward einsum operations** per sequence unit and **~0.74–0.77×
sequences/second**, summarised as **~+33% pretraining wall time** — because the
parameter budget moves out of the embedding/softmax matrices into dense layers.
Downstream *inference* penalties are much larger and task-dependent: **1.5–2.6×**
on transliteration, **3.7–6.4×** on summarisation, **6.4–9.5×** on zero-shot
XNLI, "up to 10×" worst case.

And the direction of the result depends on scale, which matters enormously here:

| GLUE avg | Small | Base | Large | XL | XXL |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| ByT5 | **80.5** | **85.3** | 87.0 | 87.9 | 90.1 |
| mT5 | 75.6 | 83.0 | **87.6** | **88.7** | **90.7** |

**Byte beats subword at Small and Base; subword wins from Large upward.** ByT5
at Large or below has roughly 300M parameters. Our entire ladder — 2M to 90M —
sits *below* the smallest published comparison point, in the regime where the
byte advantage is largest and least tested.

Other designs confirm the shape rather than contradict it: Charformer's GBST
downsamples bytes but still lost GLUE-Base to subword T5 (81.4 vs 84.3, and 83.6
for the 134M scaled variant); CANINE downsamples 4× and scored +5.7 F1 on TyDi QA
with 28% fewer parameters than mBERT; MegaByte matched a compute-controlled
Transformer at 1.02B params on 80B bytes (**1.000 vs 1.057 bits/byte**) by
patching 8 bytes per step; and [BLT](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.09871v1) reached
parity at 8B parameters on 4.5T bytes (**61.1 vs 60.0** task average against
Llama 3 1T tokens) — but with an architecture built for it.
([Charformer](https://arxiv.org/html/2106.12672v3);
[CANINE](https://arxiv.org/html/2103.06874v4);
[MegaByte](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.07185v2))

**Forced consequence.** The tokenizer is not a policy question to be blessed once
by the owner; it is an **independent variable with a published hypothesis**:
"the byte advantage inverts somewhere between Base and Large scale in subword
comparisons; where does it invert in a 2M–90M ladder on decision tasks?" That is
a cheap experiment (the 2M and 11M rungs are 60% of the byte-arm cost) and a
citable one. Recommendation: **run the ladder in both arms**, from-scratch
corpus-trained BPE and from-scratch byte-level, and report the inversion point.
Neither arm uses pretrained weights or a pretrained vocabulary.

## 5. Corrected compute arithmetic

Derived, from the measured anchors in §1.2 rather than assumed MFU. The
arithmetic lives in `scripts/budget.py` so it can be re-run rather than
re-typed.

`FLOPs ≈ 6·N·D`; H100 bf16 dense peak 989 TFLOP/s; MFU band **20–28%** from
§1.2; on-demand H100 at **$3.99/GPU-h** ([Lambda](https://lambda.ai/pricing)).

| target | N | D | 6ND | H100-h @20–28% | cost @ $3.99 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Laya **base** (what its encoder sits on) | 149M | 1.7T | 1.52e21 | 1,540–2,150 | **$6.1k–8.6k** |
| Laya **encoder**, from random | 395M | 2T | 4.74e21 | 4,760–6,660 | **$19.0k–26.6k** |
| Laya's published recipe, end to end (base→large) | — | — | 6.26e21 | 6,300–8,800 | **$25.1k–35.1k** |
| Laya **multilingual** (mmBERT class) | 307M | 3T | 5.53e21 | 5,550–7,760 | **$22.1k–31.0k** |
| Jev-class, if 1.5B × 500B (hypothetical, not disclosed) | 1.5B | 500B | 4.50e21 | 4,520–6,320 | **$18.0k–25.2k** |

Two cross-checks that validate the method: mmBERT's own reported 7,680 GPU-h
falls inside the 5,550–7,760 band predicted for 307M × 3T, and ModernBERT-base's
reported 1,552 GPU-h falls inside the 1,540–2,150 band for 149M × 1.7T. The model
reproduces two published runs before it is used to price anything else.

**The single most important correction in this document:** "Laya parity" is a
**$19k–35k** training run, not $420. It is 30–85× the figure currently in
PLAN.md §3.2, and it is *per attempt* — the base→large recipe that produced the
model we are comparing against was not one run either.

## 6. Contamination: what the standard is, and what ours found

Methods in the literature, in increasing strictness:

1. **Exact / normalised string match.** Cheap, misses paraphrase entirely.
2. **n-gram overlap.** GPT-3 used **13-gram**; PaLM used **8-gram with a 70%
   threshold per sample**. These are detector definitions, not tolerance limits.
3. **Near-duplicate / semantic retrieval** (MinHash, embeddings + adjudication).
   Reported rephrased-overlap rates on code benchmarks run **8.5%–18.9%**
   ([Yang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.04850v2)) — i.e. paraphrase
   leakage is *large* where it has been measured, which is the main limitation of
   our current checker.
4. **Behavioural probes** when the corpus is unavailable (Data Contamination
   Quiz, slot guessing).

**There is no accepted acceptable overlap rate.** A paper reporting contamination
must state the matching unit, the denominator, the flagged count, and whether
scores change when flagged examples are removed.

Our audit (2,000 probes, exact 24–64 char windows, 5,142,706 documents,
Aho-Corasick, **positive-control verified**) found **17 hits**: **15 on
wikitext-103**, 1 on xnli, 1 on arc_easy, 0 on the other 17 tasks. The
wikitext-103 result is structural — that benchmark *is* Wikipedia and our corpus
deliberately contains Wikipedia.

Per §6's standard, the correct response is **document-level decontamination, not
benchmark dropping**: identify the overlapping WikiText articles, remove those
documents from the corpus, and re-run the check. GPT-3's precedent (`omitted
four Wikipedia tasks because they were in the training data`) applies when
overlap is broad and unauditable; ours is 15/100 probes and the documents are
identifiable.

**Forced consequence.** Two work items, both cheap and both required before any
scaling claim: (a) document-level decontamination of the corpus against the one
contaminated benchmark, (b) an n-gram arm on the checker (13-gram, matching
GPT-3) since the current 24–64 char window is not the literature's standard unit.
The paraphrase blind spot gets stated in the write-up rather than papered over.

## 7. Statistics: the current gates are under-powered as written

PLAN.md's L3 gate is "beats the majority prior by ≥5 pp on ≥4 of 9 tasks". No
interval, no pairing, no correction for nine simultaneous tests.

Published guidance
([Dror et al., ACL 2018](https://aclanthology.org/P18-1128/);
[Bestgen 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2205.11134v1)):

- **Independent-sample sizing** for 75% vs 80% at α=0.05, power 0.8: **~1,094
  items per model**. Several of our nine tasks have fewer than that in test.
- **Paired** testing is far more efficient when the same items are used:
  McNemar planning gives **~312 items** at 10% discordance, **~626** at 20%,
  **~1,567** at 50%. Our rows are paired by construction (both models answer the
  same frozen rows), so paired tests are the correct instrument.
- Report **paired bootstrap CIs on the difference**, not ranks. McNemar's exact
  test when discordant counts are small.
- **ECE** must state its binning (15 equal-width is the convention) and should be
  accompanied by a reliability diagram and a binning-robust measure, because
  [Nixon et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/1904.01685) show ECE rank-orderings
  change with bin count.
- **Selective prediction** must be reported as a **risk–coverage curve**, not a
  single post-abstention accuracy
  ([Geifman & El-Yaniv](https://arxiv.org/html/1705.08500)).

**Forced consequence.** The gate becomes: *a paired bootstrap CI on the accuracy
difference that excludes zero, with a stated item count and discordance, and
Holm–Bonferroni correction across the nine primary tasks* — with the explicit
note that tasks whose test split is too small to reach power are reported as
underpowered rather than counted as wins.

## 8. Is there a defensible thesis at this budget?

**The claim in PLAN.md §1 as written is not defensible.** It costs $19k–35k per
attempt, there is no general-domain precedent for it, and its outcome is decided
by budget rather than by insight: success would show our recipe matched an
industrial 2T-token run; failure would show our budget was small. Neither is a
contribution.

**What the literature has not done, and what this machine can measure:** nobody
has published a scaling law for **decision quality**. The published laws predict
loss and transfer. Laya and Jev sell decisions — accuracy, calibrated
probabilities, abstention. Between "loss goes down predictably with compute" and
"decisions are what customers buy" there is an unmeasured gap, and it is
measurable for $0 on this machine and against a real competitor on identical
rows.

**Revised central claim:**

> Decision quality — accuracy, expected calibration error, and risk at fixed
> coverage on a frozen multi-task suite — follows a measurable scaling law in
> from-scratch pretraining compute; the law is fitted over a locally trained
> ladder and used to locate the compute at which a from-scratch model reaches
> the operating point of a 2-trillion-token pretrained encoder measured on the
> same rows.

The contribution is the curve, its fitted exponent, and the prediction interval
at the pretrained point — including if that interval excludes the possibility
that any affordable from-scratch model gets there, which is a result, not a
failure. The "beats Laya" headline becomes a **derived quantity with error bars**
instead of an assertion, and the error bars are the honest part.

Three things make this a contribution rather than an exercise:

1. **The dependent variable is new.** Loss scaling laws exist; decision scaling
   laws do not. The three decision metrics dissociate — accuracy can saturate
   while calibration keeps improving, which is exactly the regime a decision
   vendor cares about and exactly what a loss curve cannot show.
2. **The reference point is measured, not cited.** Laya is evaluated on our
   frozen rows through its own documented interface, so its point on the compute
   axis is a measurement with the same harness as ours. Its x-coordinate (2T
   tokens) is public; its y-coordinate we measure.
3. **The tokenizer sub-study is pre-registered by the literature.** ByT5's
   published inversion between Base and Large is a concrete, falsifiable
   hypothesis in a regime nobody has tested: below 300M parameters, on decision
   tasks, from scratch.

**What this thesis cannot claim, stated up front:** it does not produce a model
that beats Laya. It produces the measurement that says what beating Laya costs,
and it will be reported that way in the abstract, the first paragraph, and
every table caption.
